7 gaps to check on your Colorado Springs homeowners policy

By Greg Strauch, Farmers agent, Colorado license #921706 · Published November 3, 2026

Most people don't find out what their home policy doesn't cover until they file a claim. Pull up your declarations page and check these seven things. They're the ones that matter most in Colorado Springs, where hail, wildfire and rebuild costs drive most of the big surprises.

What two of these gaps cost together

An illustrative example with round numbers, not a real client or claim.

  • June: a hailstorm damages the 16-year-old roof on a home insured for $500,000. The roofer's estimate to replace it is $24,000.
  • The policy: a 2% wind/hail deductible ($10,000) and a roof covered at actual cash value.
  • The math: the insurer depreciates the roof by half for its age, so the roof is valued at $12,000. Subtract the $10,000 deductible.
  • The check: $2,000 toward a $24,000 roof. The owner pays $22,000.
  • The same storm with a 1% deductible and replacement cost on the roof: $24,000 minus $5,000 is a $19,000 check. The owner pays $5,000.

Both gaps were printed on the declarations page the whole time. Not sure where to look? Here is how to read your declarations page.

1. A wind/hail deductible you can't pay

Most Front Range policies now carry a separate wind and hail deductible, often 1–2% of your dwelling coverage. On a $500,000 house that's $5,000–$10,000 out of pocket per storm. El Paso County had hail on 138 days from 2016 through 2025, more than any other Colorado county (see the data), so this is the deductible you're most likely to actually pay.

Check: the wind/hail deductible line on your declarations page. Is it a percentage? What's that in dollars?

2. Dwelling coverage based on market value instead of rebuild cost

Your dwelling limit should be what it costs to rebuild your house today with today's labor and materials, not what it would sell for or what you paid. Construction costs in El Paso County have risen faster than home values, so a limit set years ago can fall well short.

Check: when your dwelling limit was last updated, and whether it came from a replacement-cost estimate.

3. A roof paid at actual cash value

Some policies pay to replace a damaged roof at full cost. Others pay actual cash value, which subtracts depreciation for the roof's age. On a 15-year-old roof that can mean a much smaller check. Roof age and material also move your premium, and impact-resistant shingles are one of the few things that can bring it down.

Check: whether your roof is covered at replacement cost or actual cash value, and how old the insurer thinks it is.

4. No water backup or sump coverage

A standard homeowners policy doesn't cover water that backs up through a drain or overflows a sump pump. It's usually an inexpensive add-on, and it's the uncovered claim we see most often.

Check: for a "water backup" or "sump overflow" endorsement and its limit.

5. Valuables that aren't scheduled

Standard policies cap theft coverage for categories like jewelry, firearms and collectibles at fairly low amounts. Engagement rings, gun collections, musical instruments and collectibles usually need to be listed ("scheduled") separately to be covered at full value.

Check: the special limits section of your policy, and whether anything you own is worth more than those limits.

6. Liability limits that don't match your auto policy

If your home liability is $100,000 and your auto liability is $300,000, the low one is your weak spot. The two should match, and if you have real assets to protect, an umbrella policy sits on top of both.

Check: personal liability on your home policy against bodily injury liability on your auto policy.

7. No flood coverage near a creek or burn scar

Homeowners policies exclude flood. If you're near Fountain Creek or Monument Creek, or downhill from a burn scar, it's worth asking about. The 2013 flash flooding off the Waldo Canyon burn scar hit homes nobody thought were at risk.

Check: whether you have a separate flood policy. Flood policies usually have a waiting period before coverage starts, so don't wait for a forecast.

Own a condo?

Add one more. Your HOA's master policy covers the building, but if the HOA has a big claim you can be assessed a share of its deductible. Loss assessment coverage on your HO-6 policy covers that, and it's the piece condo owners most often leave out.
